Build Your Own Personal Chief of StaffAgenda
The Problem

Four tabs and a memory test, every morning

  • Open email — scan for anything urgent
  • Open calendar — check what meetings need prep
  • Open Slack — check what you missed overnight
  • Try to remember what you promised, and what someone promised you
Why It's Tiring

None of this is hard.

It's just scattered, repetitive, and it never stops. The triage tax accumulates daily whether or not the work itself is difficult.

One Project, four things it knows about you

1

Who you are

Your role, your priority people, what "urgent" actually means for your job

2

How you sound

Your writing voice, so anything drafted sounds like you — not a generic assistant

3

Your real tools

Live, read access to your inbox and calendar via Connectors

4

What it's learned

Memory — corrections and commitments that stick without redoing your setup

Why A Project, Not Just A Chat

Persistence is the whole trick

A Regular Chat
  • Starts from zero every time
  • You re-explain your role and priorities each time
  • No access to your real inbox/calendar unless you paste it in
  • Nothing you correct persists to next time
A Claude Project
  • Custom instructions persist across every conversation
  • Connectors give live, read access to real systems
  • Memory keeps what it learns as you use it
  • A two-word prompt like "morning briefing" produces a real answer

Two Different Tools

Custom instructions vs. memory

Custom instructionsMemory
What it holdsStable facts about you — role, priority people, voice, standing rulesThings that happened — a correction, a commitment, a one-off preference
How it's writtenYou write or paste it yourself, deliberatelyIt writes itself as you talk to it, or you say "remember this"
Good for"I am the VP of Ops" / "Always draft, never send""I promised the report by Friday" / "Ignore emails from that newsletter"
ChangesRarely — revisit occasionallyConstantly — accumulates as you use it

Rule of thumb: if it's true every day, it goes in custom instructions. If it's true this week, let memory hold it.

Build Your Own Personal Chief of StaffConcept
Boundaries

What this is not

  • Not autonomous. It never sends an email or message on your behalf without you seeing and approving it first. It drafts. You decide.
  • Not shared. Your Project only sees what YOUR connected accounts can see. Nobody else's data, nobody else's inbox.
  • Not magic. If you don't tell it what matters to you, it will guess — and generic guesses are exactly what we're avoiding. The setup work is what makes the difference.

Identity + Voice

Teach it who you are and how you sound

Build Your Own Personal Chief of StaffPart 2
Exercise 1 · 10 min

Identity and priorities

  1. Name, role, company. One line each — the easy win, builds momentum.
  2. Who matters most. 3-5 people whose messages you'd drop everything for. Not your whole org chart.
  3. Define "urgent" for your job specifically. Be concrete: "anything from my manager about a live issue," not "time-sensitive things."
  4. Paste it all into Custom Instructions and save. This is the main file — every Project has exactly one Custom Instructions box.
Checkpoint

Open a new chat and ask:

"What do you know about me so far?"

It should reflect your name, role, and priority people back to you.

Build Your Own Personal Chief of StaffPart 2
Exercise 2 · 10 min

Voice

  1. Pick your spectrum. Direct vs. warm. Short vs. detailed. Formal vs. casual.
  2. Write 3 rules in your own words. Turn what you picked into checkable instructions.
  3. Paste one real example. A real email or message you've actually written — this teaches tone better than any adjective list.
  4. Add it to the same Custom Instructions box. Or attach it as a separate file — see the next slide for that choice.
Checkpoint

Ask it to draft a one-line Slack message about a meeting moving to 3pm.

Does it sound like you? If not, sharpen the rules or add another example.

How The Pieces Fit Together

One main file. Everything else is optional.

The Main File

custom-instructions-template.md

Pasted directly into the Project's Custom Instructions box. Every Project has exactly one. Think of it the same way you'd think of a CLAUDE.md or agent.md — the one file that defines who the assistant is.

Add-on — Voice

voice-template.md

Attach as a separate knowledge file, or merge its content into the main file's "My Voice" section. Recommended, not required.

Add-on — Preferences

config-template.md

Calendar habits, approvals, commitment tracking. Optional — add later once the basics work.

I want you to figure out my writing voice by analyzing real examples I give you, then write it up as a clear style guide I can hand to another AI assistant so it writes like me. Alternative Path · Prompt 2 of 2

Find my priorities — copy this into a plain Claude chat

No prep needed — this one works as a pure interview.

I want to set up a personal AI assistant (a Chief of Staff) and I need to define who I am and what matters to me so it can prioritize correctly. Interview me with short, one-at-a-time questions — don't ask everything at once. Cover: 1. My name, role, and company/team 2. The 3-5 people whose messages should always get flagged first (ask me who they are and why) 3. What "urgent" actually means in my specific job — push me for something concrete, not generic ("time-sensitive things") 4. How I want things categorized when it scans my email or messages (offer URGENT / NEEDS RESPONSE / FYI / CAN WAIT as a default) 5. Anything it should NEVER do automatically (e.g. never send without my review) Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer before moving to the next. Once you have all 5, write it up as a finished markdown document titled "identity.md" with clear headers matching the 5 areas above.

Morning briefing

"Give me a morning briefing: what's on my calendar today, and what in my inbox needs my attention. Sort by urgency using the categories in my instructions."
What Should Happen

It reads your real, connected Gmail and Calendar and returns a categorized summary. Approve any connector permission prompt if it appears.

If Categorization Looks Wrong

Expected on the first try. Tell it directly why, and say "remember this" if you want the correction kept — more on this in Part 4.

Part 4

Teach It With Memory

The difference between a demo and something that gets better

Build Your Own Personal Chief of StaffPart 4
Exercise 4 · 10 min

Save it, then prove it stuck

Step 1

Explicit save

Say: "Remember that I never want meeting invites before 9am."

Open a new chat in the same Project. Ask what it remembers. Confirm it comes back without repeating it.

Step 2

Correction as memory

Go back to something Exercise 3 got wrong. Instead of editing custom instructions, just say "remember that—" and explain the correction like you would to a person.

Build Your Own Personal Chief of StaffPart 4
One Thing To Watch

Memory can be wrong, or go stale

It might "remember" that a preference always applies when it was really a one-time thing.

"That's no longer the case, forget it."  /  "Update that — it's actually X now."

If it recalls something that's no longer true, just say so — same as correcting a person.

Go Further

Advanced moves once the basics feel natural

  • Draft replies in your voice. Not just summarizing — actually writing, in your tone, ready for your review.
  • Track commitments with memory. "Remember I owe Deja the report by Friday" — no separate tracking doc needed.
  • Meeting prep. Pull context on attendees from recent email and calendar history before a call.
  • Connect Slack. Read and draft there too, using the same identity and voice.

Anticipated Questions

Have these answers ready

  • Does this see my email forever / train on it? No — Connectors give live read access during use. Confirm your org's specific data-retention answer with IT before presenting.
  • Can it send things without me approving? No — this pattern always drafts, never sends. Worth repeating twice during the session.
  • Can other people see what it remembers about me? No — memory is scoped to your own account/Project, same as custom instructions.
  • What if it remembers something wrong? Tell it directly — "forget that" or "that's changed, update it" — same as correcting a person.
